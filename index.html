Ch. 1Diagnosis

What you notice, and what's usually underneath it.

Most of the organizations I talk with describe the same few symptoms. They feel like separate problems. They usually share a root.

Symptom: what you noticeWhat's underneath
1.a"Every push is tied to an event, a deadline, or a crisis. Then it goes quiet." Reactive instead of strategic.There's no year-round plan, so the calendar decides what gets asked for, and when. Fundraising happens in bursts, and so does burnout.
1.b"People give once, maybe twice, and then we lose track of them." Donor relationships stall.Nobody has mapped what should happen after a first gift, so commitment never gets a clear path to deepen.
1.c"Our executive director is also our development department." Too much work, too few hands.A full-time development director is out of reach, so strategy gets squeezed in between everything else, and the writing never stops.
1.d"The board is enthusiastic. Nothing really moves." Board energy without direction.Members want to help but haven't been given clear expectations, the right tools, or a defined role in the plan.
1.e"Half the team uses ChatGPT. Nobody's sure what's okay to paste into it." AI without guardrails.The tools are already in use, with no policy for donor data and no shared sense of where AI helps fundraising and where it doesn't.

Ch. 5Common questions

Questions people ask before we start.

How can AI help a small fundraising team?

Mostly by taking on the writing and research load: donor research briefs before meetings, first drafts of thank-you letters, appeals, and grant narratives, meeting notes, and data cleanup. The goal is more of your team's time with donors. The AI Fundraising Sprint sets this up for your team.

Is it safe to use AI with donor information?

It can be, with guardrails. Decide up front what never goes into an AI tool, which tools are approved, and where a person always reviews the output. Every AI engagement I do includes a written policy that covers donor data.

What is an associate board, and how is it different from a board of directors?

A board of directors governs the organization and carries legal and fiduciary responsibility. An associate board is a separate group of committed supporters, often emerging leaders, who raise money, advocate, and expand your network without governance duties. A well-designed associate board can also become a pipeline for future board members and major donors.

What size organizations do you work with?

Mostly small nonprofits where fundraising rests on a few people: an executive director doing development on the side, a development team of one to three, or a board that wants to help. My own fundraising experience spans organizations with budgets from $700,000 to $450 million.
